The Performance Testing Judgment Pack

Heuristics, checklists, and templates for the decisions before a load test, the traps in the results after it, and defending the verdict.

Performance testing is mostly judgment. This pack is the judgment, written down — for working performance testers: how to explain results to leadership, how to get teams to actually do this work, and how to review AI-generated tests and analysis before you sign them. Files, not a course. Read them next to a real run.

Who this is for

  • Performance testers and quality engineers who can already run a load test and want the results to hold up under scrutiny
  • SREs and platform engineers who review other teams' test results and have to say yes or no
  • Team leads who need to explain a test result upward and defend the verdict
  • Anyone using AI tools to draft performance tests or reports who wants a verification discipline before trusting the output

Who this is NOT for

  • Not a course. No videos, no lessons, no cohort, nothing to attend. You buy files, you read them, you apply them.
  • Not tool mechanics. It will not teach k6 syntax or what a VU is. Grafana's documentation teaches that well, and it is free. This pack starts where the documentation stops.
  • Not a beginner's introduction. It assumes you have run tests and read real output.

What's in the pack

  1. Before the test — decisions and workload modeling. Which test type answers the question you actually have, and when each type is the wrong tool. Workload modeling, VU calculation with Little's Law (both methods, and how to choose), and a decision tree for what to test right now.
  2. After the test — reading results without fooling yourself. The traps in load-test output, named: checks read before failure rates, why 0% failures with half your checks failing is not a partial pass, the threshold checkmark that means almost nothing, averages dragged down by fast failures, percentiles built on too little data, and measurement windows that exclude ramp noise. (This is the free sample chapter, below.)
  3. Explaining results to leadership. A two-tier reporting system — a verdict-first summary in plain language over a technical evidence page — with acceptance bands and a watch-items format. Plus frameworks for turning metrics into decisions and dollars, by audience level. These frameworks are research-backed and labeled as such: the industry's mapped approaches to a hard conversation, presented honestly, not dressed up as anyone's personal pitch history.
  4. Getting teams to actually do it — enablement and governance. Why testing mandates produce checkbox compliance, why quality gates on practices people don't understand get edited instead of obeyed, and what works instead: a smart-check cadence policy (not test-every-deploy), per-deploy risk scoring, and building the standard into the platform so doing it right is the easy path.
  5. Reviewing AI-generated tests and analysis. An eight-point checklist for verifying an AI-drafted test design and an AI-drafted report, in order. What AI gets wrong in stage design and why, how to align the data source and the question, and how to make the AI verify its own work so the human keeps the understanding and the verdict instead of redoing the arithmetic.
